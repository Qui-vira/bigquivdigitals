# Nobody publishes what it costs to run a business in Nigeria. So I built it.

Eight government agencies publish what things cost here. None of them publish it together. I did, and then spent most of the project making sure it was honest.

## The problem

Your costs went up. Which ones, and by how much?

Almost no business in Nigeria can answer that. The prices are public. Diesel, petrol, cooking gas, bus fares, food, electricity, inflation. But eight different agencies publish them, in eight different formats, on eight different dates, and nobody puts them in one place.

So you feel the squeeze without ever seeing where it came from. Diesel more than doubled in eight months. Most owners never saw that number.

## What it does

It puts all eight in one place and answers three questions.

**What went up, and by how much?**

**Does it depend on where you are?**

**Should a business like mine care?**

Nine costs, month by month, across all 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory.

## Where the numbers come from

The obvious way to build this is to download all eight, line them up by state and month, and put a dashboard on top. It looks convincing and part of it is invented, because **three of the eight never publish state-by-state figures at all.** Food comes by region. Electricity comes by supply company. The exchange rate is one national number. Forcing those into a table with a row per state means inventing the gaps.

So I only combined the figures that genuinely match, left the rest at the level it was published, and labelled every number with which level it came from.

**342 source files, 1.6 GB, every one checked by hash** when it was downloaded and again after every step. Straight from the agencies, no mirrors. Where a file was never published, the gap stays a gap.

I read all 219 spreadsheets and 173 PDFs before writing a line of code, because the traps do not announce themselves:

- **Headings move.** The same monthly report puts its column names on row 1, row 3 or row 16 depending on the month. Code that assumes row 1 reads a sentence as a column name and carries on.
- **The petrol sheet is two tables side by side.** One is states, the other is regions, and nothing on the page says so.
- **One transport file labels two columns with the same month.** The second is really the month after. I proved it by checking 33 states' values against the next release.

Miss any of them and the numbers come out wrong while the dashboard still looks fine.

## The data model

Everything goes into **PostgreSQL**: 29,032 rows of figures across 12 tables, with 30 ready-made views on top for reporting.

The **four levels of geography stay apart** instead of being flattened into one state table: state, region, national, and electricity supply area. Every figure carries a label saying which level it came from, so a chart can never show detail the source never had.

Some rules are built into the database rather than written down in a guide and hoped for. The statistics bureau prints a warning inside its own spreadsheet: its state price indexes must not be used to rank states, because every state's shopping basket is different. That warning rides on all 6,512 of those rows, and neither dashboard can break it. Excel leaves those two figures out of its data entirely. Power BI returns a blank instead of a number.

In Power BI, six links between tables are **missing on purpose.** Food has no state-level prices, so the link that would let someone chart food by state does not exist to be misused.

## The Excel workbook

Seven sheets, each named for the question it answers rather than for the dataset behind it. What is changing · Fuel and power · Moving people and goods · Where costs differ · Is this month's price move unusual · Who this matters more for · Can I trust this.

Every sheet leads with the conclusion in plain English, then shows the chart underneath as evidence, then states what the data cannot tell you.

## What the data actually showed

**Diesel more than doubled.** Up 158% from its low point in September 2025 to May 2026, and it went up in all 37 states. Even the slowest rose 115%.

**Falling fuel prices did not reach transport.** In 81 cases where petrol was cheaper than a year earlier, fares still went up: motorcycle taxis and water transport in 100% of them, city buses in 98.8%. Fares ratchet. They rise with fuel and then stay up.

**The inflation rate in the news was going the other way.** It fell from about 22% to about 15% over the same period, while okada fares rose about 51%. A business that raised its transport allowances in line with the headline inflation rate fell roughly 35 percentage points behind what it was actually paying.

**Running a generator was never cheaper than the grid.** Diesel self-generation cost between 3.9 and 5.2 times the reference electricity tariff, in every month of the series. A generator is cover for power cuts, not a cheaper supply.

**Where you are matters hugely for some costs and barely at all for others.** Boat fares are almost seven times higher in the dearest state than the cheapest. Petrol differs by 14%, which is close to nothing. Choosing between two places on fuel price is choosing on almost nothing.

**For five of the nine costs, the cheapest place keeps changing.** In a normal month a price moves more than the entire gap between the cheapest state and the dearest, so the order shuffles. Each month's ranking is still right for that month. What it cannot support is calling one place the cheapest over time, so it refuses to name one.

## What a business owner gets from it

Not an instruction. The dataset holds no company's cost shares, margins or ability to pass a price rise on, so it cannot tell any particular business what to do.

What it can do is narrow the question. Find the kind of business closest to yours, see which of the nine costs it actually buys in volume, and see what those costs have done. Then go and measure the one thing the project cannot see: how much of your own spending goes on it.

If diesel is a large share of what you spend, a 158% rise is a serious event. If it is a small share, the same figure barely touches you. Same number, two completely different conclusions, and the difference is a figure only you hold.

## What it does not claim

This is the part I spent the most time on, because it is where a cost dashboard turns into something dishonest.

It measures nine costs a business buys in, plus inflation rates. **That is not the cost of doing business.** There is no rent, no wages, no land, no taxes, no levies and no stock in it.

It does **not** predict whether a company will make or lose money, and it does **not** identify the best Nigerian state for business. A cheaper state is not a better one. Whether a business does well somewhere also depends on customer demand, purchasing power, market size, competition, rent, wages, supplier access and infrastructure. None of that is in this data.

Those limits are not a footnote. Both tools carry them on two pages each, and the build fails if a page ever claims otherwise. An automatic check reads every line of text in the report and rejects phrases like "best state" or "cheaper place to do business" before anything can ship.

## How I know it is right

**110 of 110 analysis checks pass**, across three separate passes. The database has its own audit at **245 of 245**, which re-reads all 17 source files from scratch rather than trusting what was loaded.

Both dashboards are checked against the finished file, not against the build log:

- **Excel: 105 checks, plus 25 more that open it in real Microsoft Excel.** Every headline figure is worked out again and compared against the evidence behind it. The file is fingerprinted before and after, and the two have to match, which proves the checker only read it.
- **Power BI: 89 checks.** It opens the report in Power BI Desktop, clears the cached data so the refresh is real, then asks the running report for its own numbers.

That caution was earned. An early version of the workbook shipped 20 broken values hidden inside the file, and every build log had called it a success. A log saying "written" is not proof.

Nothing is quietly patched over. Eleven government releases that were due and never appeared stay missing, and the page names them. Where a figure has no earlier month to compare against, the line breaks instead of guessing across the gap.

## Built with

Python and pandas for the cleaning pipelines. PostgreSQL for the model and the analytical mart. SQL for the analysis. Power BI, as a text-based project rather than a binary file. Excel, generated and validated by script. Git throughout, with every stage committed as it was proved.

The repository holds the pipelines, the schema, the analysis scripts, the validation suites and both dashboards: [github.com/Qui-vira/nigeria-business-cost-intelligence](https://github.com/Qui-vira/nigeria-business-cost-intelligence).

## Why this matters to you

Most dashboards are built to look certain. This one is built to be checkable, and it says out loud where it stops.

If you need data work where the numbers survive someone actually testing them, this is what that looks like.
