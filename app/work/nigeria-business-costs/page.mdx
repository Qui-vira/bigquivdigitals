# Your business costs are rising. Can you prove which ones are killing you?

Running a business gets more expensive, but knowing exactly **what changed, where, and by how much** is surprisingly difficult.

The data exists.

Diesel. Petrol. Cooking gas. Transport fares. Food. Electricity. Inflation.

But it is scattered across eight different government sources, published in different formats, at different times, and at different levels of geography.

So I brought it together.

Not to calculate the total cost of running a business in Nigeria. The available data cannot honestly do that.

The goal was simpler:

**Show how major external business costs are changing, where they differ, and which ones a business may need to pay attention to.**

## The problem

Your costs went up.

But which ones?

And by how much?

Maybe diesel became more expensive. Maybe transport did. Maybe electricity, petrol, food or cooking gas moved enough to affect what you spend every month.

The problem is that seeing those changes together is difficult.

The numbers are public, but they live in separate reports from different agencies, using different structures and release schedules.

That leaves business owners feeling the increase without having a clear picture of where it came from.

One example is diesel.

From its low point in September 2025 to May 2026, the median increase across Nigeria's 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory was **158.2%**.

That is the kind of change a business should be able to see clearly.

## So I built one place to see it

Nigeria Business Cost Intelligence brings these datasets together and tries to answer three practical questions:

**What went up, and by how much?**

**Does the answer change depending on where you are?**

**Is this a cost a business like mine should pay attention to?**

The project tracks nine major cost categories over time across all 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory where state-level data actually exists.

But putting the data together turned out to be the easy part.

The harder part was making sure the result did not quietly say things the original data never said.

## The data does not all fit together

The obvious approach would be to download every dataset, match everything by state and month, and build a dashboard.

That would look clean.

It would also be wrong.

Three of the eight sources do not publish state-level figures at all.

Food is published by region.

Electricity is published by electricity supply area.

The exchange rate is national.

If I forced those numbers into a table containing one row for every state, I would be creating geographic detail that never existed in the source.

So I did not.

Each figure stays at the level at which it was actually published: **state, region, national, or electricity supply area.**

Nothing is pushed down to a lower level simply because it makes the dashboard easier to build.

That same rule applies to missing data.

The project contains **342 raw source files, roughly 1.6 GB in total**. Every file is verified using SHA-256 hashing when it is downloaded and again after processing.

The sources come directly from the original publishers. No Kaggle copies. No mirrors. No aggregators.

And when an official file is missing, the project records the gap instead of filling it with an invented value.

## Then came the messy part

Before writing the transformation pipelines, I profiled **219 spreadsheet sheets and 173 PDFs**.

That exposed problems that would be easy to miss if the files were simply loaded automatically.

NBS header rows, for example, are not consistently placed. Across different releases, they appear on rows 1, 2, 3, 4, 15 and 16.

A script that assumes the first row contains the column names works for some files and quietly breaks others.

Petrol has another problem.

One worksheet contains two different tables side by side. Columns 1 to 4 contain state data, while columns 6 and 7 contain zones with completely different meanings.

Diesel mixes states, zones and the national total inside one unnamed column.

Cooking gas contains separate tables for 5 kg and 12.5 kg cylinders using identical column names. The only reliable way to distinguish them is their position in the sheet, and even those positions changed in 2026.

One transport file labels two columns with the same period even though the second column actually belongs to the following March. I verified that by comparing values across 33 states with the next month's official release.

Any one of these problems could quietly corrupt an automated pipeline.

So the system was designed around the source files as they actually exist, not around how convenient I wanted them to be.

## The database keeps those differences intact

Once cleaned, the data lands in **PostgreSQL**.

The database contains **29,032 fact rows across 12 core fact tables**, with 30 analytical mart objects built on top.

The important part is not the row count.

It is what the model refuses to combine.

State data stays state data.

Regional data stays regional.

National data stays national.

Electricity supply areas stay electricity supply areas.

Every figure carries its geographic level with it.

That prevents a chart from implying more detail than the source actually provides.

Some restrictions go even further.

The national statistics source explicitly warns that its state price indexes should not be used to rank states by cost because each state's basket is different.

That warning is attached to all **6,512 relevant rows**.

The dashboards are also prevented from violating it.

Those index measures are excluded from the Excel data layer completely. In Power BI, the corresponding measure returns blank instead of allowing an invalid comparison.

The model even contains **six missing relationships on purpose**.

For example, food has no state-level prices, so the relationship that would allow someone to chart food by state simply does not exist.

The model cannot accidentally answer a question the source data cannot support.

## Then I built the Excel workbook

The workbook contains seven sheets.

Instead of naming them after datasets, each one is named after the question someone using the data is actually trying to answer:

**What is changing**

**Fuel and power**

**Moving people and goods**

**Where costs differ**

**Is this month's price move unusual**

**Who this matters more for**

**Can I trust this**

Every sheet follows the same pattern.

First, it states the finding in plain English.

Then it shows the chart or figures behind that finding.

Then it explains what the data cannot tell you.

## What the data showed

### Diesel more than doubled

From its low point in September 2025 to May 2026, diesel rose by a median **158.2%** across the 37 jurisdictions.

Every jurisdiction recorded an increase.

Even the smallest increase was **115%**.

The chart title does not make you interpret the line before understanding the point.

It tells you:

**Every fuel cost here ended the period higher than it started.**

### Falling petrol prices did not automatically mean cheaper transport

There were **81 cases** where petrol was cheaper than it had been a year earlier.

Transport fares often did not follow it down.

Motorcycle taxi and water transport fares were still higher in **100% of those cases**.

City bus fares were higher in **98.8%**.

Fuel costs can fall without the transport prices businesses and customers actually pay falling with them.

### Headline inflation did not describe every cost pressure

Over the period examined, the headline inflation rate fell from roughly **22% to 15%**.

At the same time, motorcycle taxi fares rose by roughly **51%**.

A business using headline inflation alone to adjust transport allowances would therefore have been roughly **35 percentage points behind** the change in that particular transport cost.

The national inflation number was moving one way while a cost businesses actually pay was moving another.

### Generators were consistently more expensive than the reference grid tariff

Across every month in the series, estimated diesel self-generation cost between **3.9 and 5.2 times** the reference electricity tariff.

The result is simple.

A generator may be necessary when grid power is unavailable, but in this dataset it was never the cheaper source of electricity.

### Location matters far more for some costs than others

Water transport fares differed by almost **seven times** between the lowest and highest jurisdictions.

Petrol differed by only about **1.14 times**.

So saying that "location matters" without asking **which cost** hides most of the useful information.

For some costs, geography changes the picture dramatically.

For others, the difference is relatively small.

And even when a place is cheaper in one month, that does not make it permanently cheaper.

For five of the nine costs, the lowest-cost jurisdiction keeps changing.

In those cases, a single month's movement can be larger than the entire gap between the lowest and highest jurisdiction.

So the ranking reshuffles.

The project can show what was cheapest in a particular month.

It does not pretend that one jurisdiction is permanently the cheapest.

## What does any of this mean for an actual business?

The project deliberately stops short of telling a business owner what to do.

It cannot see your company's margins.

It does not know how much diesel you use.

It does not know what percentage of your costs goes to transport.

It does not know whether you can pass a price increase on to your customers.

So a national number by itself cannot tell you how serious a change is for your particular company.

Instead, the project helps narrow the question.

Find the type of business closest to yours.

Look at the costs that kind of business is likely to buy heavily.

See how those costs have moved.

Then compare that with the number the public datasets cannot know:

**How much of your own spending goes there?**

A **158% increase in diesel** could be a major event for a company that depends heavily on generators and logistics.

For a business that barely buys diesel, the same national change could have little effect.

Same external number.

Different business.

Different consequence.

## That is also why I do not call this "the cost of doing business"

The project tracks nine external costs businesses may buy, plus inflation measures.

That is not the same thing as measuring the complete cost of running a business.

There is no rent.

No wages.

No land.

No taxes or levies.

No inventory costs.

Because those things are absent, the project does not claim to predict whether a company will make or lose money.

It also does not identify the "best state for business."

A location having cheaper fuel or transport does not automatically make it a better place to operate.

A business also depends on customer demand, purchasing power, market size, competition, supplier access, infrastructure, wages, rent and many other factors that are outside this dataset.

Those limitations are part of the system, not a disclaimer added at the bottom.

They are stored as data in both tools.

They appear directly inside the dashboards.

And the build contains language checks that reject claims such as "best state" or "cheaper place to do business" before the final output can ship.

## Then I tested whether I could trust my own work

This became one of the largest parts of the project.

The analysis layer passes **110 out of 110 checks** across three separate validation passes.

The PostgreSQL database has an independent audit containing **245 out of 245 passing checks**.

That audit does not simply trust what was loaded into the database.

It goes back to the **17 source CSV files**, parses them again from scratch, and reconciles the result independently.

The dashboards are tested separately too.

The Excel workbook goes through **105 structural and reconciliation checks**, plus **25 Microsoft Excel compatibility checks**.

Every headline number is recalculated and compared with its committed evidence.

The workbook is hashed before and after validation. The two hashes must match, proving the validator inspected the file without altering it.

The Power BI report goes through another **89 checks**.

The process opens the project in Power BI Desktop, removes the existing data cache, performs a genuine refresh, and interrogates the live model directly.

That level of validation came from learning the hard way that a successful build message proves very little.

An early workbook passed its build process while still containing **20 invalid numeric values inside its XML**.

The logs said the workbook had been written successfully.

The file itself was wrong.

That changed the standard for the rest of the project.

A tool saying "success" is not proof.

The output has to survive independent testing.

## Why I built it this way

The useful part of a dashboard is not how certain it looks.

It is whether the numbers still hold up when someone starts asking questions.

Where did this figure come from?

Was that geography actually published?

What happens when a source file is missing?

Can I reproduce the calculation?

What does this number **not** prove?

Nigeria Business Cost Intelligence was built so those questions have answers.

Not to make the data look cleaner than it is.

Not to turn incomplete information into certainty.

But to take scattered public data, make it usable, and make sure every conclusion stops exactly where the evidence stops.

If you need data work where the numbers are expected to survive someone actually testing them, this is what that looks like.
