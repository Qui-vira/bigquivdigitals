# Nobody publishes what it costs to run a business in Nigeria. So I built it.

Diesel, petrol, cooking gas, transport fares, food prices, electricity tariffs, the exchange rate and inflation. Eight government agencies publish these. Every one of them publishes separately, on its own schedule, in its own file format.

No one publishes the combined picture. I did, and then I spent most of the project making sure it was honest.

## The problem

A shop owner in Lagos knows this month cost more than last month. Ask them which cost went up, and by how much, and nobody can answer.

The figures do exist. Every month the government publishes the price of diesel, petrol, cooking gas, bus fares, food and electricity, and the inflation rate on top of that. The trouble is that eight different agencies publish them, on eight different timetables, in eight different file layouts. Nobody puts them side by side.

So you never get to see that diesel more than doubled while the inflation rate on the news was falling. You only feel that things got harder.

**The obvious fix does not work.** Download all eight, line them up by state and by month, put a dashboard on top. It looks convincing, and part of it is made up, because **three of the eight never publish state-by-state figures at all.** Food is published by region. Electricity is published by supply company. The exchange rate is a single national number. To force those into a table with one row per state, you have to invent the missing numbers and hope nobody checks.

I did the slower version. Combine only the figures that genuinely line up. Leave the rest at the level it was actually published. And on every single number, say which level it is, so a chart can never suggest detail that was never there in the first place.

## What it does

It follows the outside costs a Nigerian business pays, and answers three questions.

**What changed, and by how much?** Nine costs, tracked month by month.

**Does it depend on where you are?** For some costs it matters enormously. For others it barely matters at all, and the report says which is which rather than leaving you to guess.

**Does this affect a business like mine?** Pick the closest type of business, see which of the nine costs it actually buys in any volume, and get a short list of things to check in your own books.

The nine are the ones that can honestly be compared state by state: diesel, petrol, two sizes of cooking gas cylinder, and five ways of moving people and goods. Inflation rates sit alongside them, across all 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory.

## Where the numbers come from

**342 raw source files, roughly 1.6 GB, every one verified by SHA-256 hash** at download and after every processing stage. No Kaggle, no mirrors, no aggregators. Where an official file does not exist, the gap is recorded instead of filled.

Before writing any transformation code I profiled **219 spreadsheet sheets and 173 PDFs**, because the traps are not obvious:

- NBS header rows move between files. They appear on rows 1, 2, 3, 4, 15 and 16 across different months. Code that assumes row 1 reads a title sentence as column names, for some months and not others, and fails silently.
- The petrol sheet holds two different tables side by side. Columns 1 to 4 are states; columns 6 and 7 are zones with different row meanings.
- The diesel sheet mixes states, zones and a national total in one unnamed column, so selecting "all rows" returns three geographic levels at once.
- Cooking gas has two cylinder-size tables with identical headers, told apart only by column position, and the columns moved in 2026.
- One transport file labels two columns with the same period. The second is really the following March, which I proved by cross-checking 33 states' values against the next month's release.

Each one would have quietly corrupted a naive pipeline.

## The data model

Everything lands in **PostgreSQL**: 29,032 fact rows across 12 core fact tables, with 30 objects in an analytical mart layer on top.

The model keeps **four levels of geography apart** rather than flattening them into one state table: state, region, national, and electricity supply area. Every single figure carries a label saying which of the four it came from, so a chart can never imply detail the source never published.

Some rules are enforced by the database and the model rather than by documentation. The national bureau prints, inside its own source table, that its state price indexes must not be used to rank states by cost, because each state's basket is different. That warning is carried on all 6,512 of those rows, and the dashboards physically cannot break it: the two index measures are excluded from the workbook's data layer entirely, and in the Power BI model the measure returns blank rather than a number.

Six relationships in the Power BI model are **missing on purpose**. Food has no state-level prices, so the join that would let someone chart food by state does not exist to be used.

## The Excel workbook

Seven sheets, each named for the question it answers rather than for the dataset behind it. What is changing · Fuel and power · Moving people and goods · Where costs differ · Is this month's price move unusual · Who this matters more for · Can I trust this.

Every sheet leads with the conclusion in plain English, then shows the chart underneath as evidence, then states what the data cannot tell you.

## What the data actually showed

**Diesel more than doubled.** Up a median 158.2% from its low point in September 2025 to May 2026, and it rose in all 37 jurisdictions. The slowest of them still rose 115%.

**Falling fuel prices did not reach transport.** In 81 cases where petrol was cheaper than a year earlier, fares still went up: motorcycle taxis and water transport in 100% of them, city buses in 98.8%. Fares ratchet. They rise with fuel and then stay up.

**The inflation rate in the news was going the other way.** It fell from about 22% to about 15% over the same period, while okada fares rose about 51%. A business that raised its transport allowances in line with the headline inflation rate fell roughly 35 percentage points behind what it was actually paying.

**Running a generator was never cheaper than the grid.** Diesel self-generation cost between 3.9 and 5.2 times the reference electricity tariff, in every month of the series. A generator is cover for power cuts, not a cheaper supply.

**Where you are matters enormously for some costs and barely at all for others.** Water transport fares differ almost sevenfold between the cheapest and dearest jurisdiction. Petrol differs by 1.14 times, which is close to noise. Comparing two locations on fuel is comparing almost nothing.

**For five of the nine costs, the cheapest place keeps changing.** A single month's price move is usually bigger than the whole gap between the cheapest and dearest jurisdiction, so the order reshuffles. Each month's ranking is still correct for that month. What the data will not support is naming one place the cheapest over time, so it refuses to name one.

## What a business owner gets from it

Not an instruction. The dataset holds no company's cost shares, margins or ability to pass a price rise on, so it cannot tell any particular business what to do.

What it can do is narrow the question. Find the kind of business closest to yours, see which of the nine costs it actually buys in volume, and see what those costs have done. Then go and measure the one thing the project cannot see: how much of your own spending goes on it.

If diesel is a large share of what you spend, a 158% rise is a serious event. If it is a small share, the same figure barely touches you. Same number, two completely different conclusions, and the difference is a figure only you hold.

## What it does not claim

This is the part I spent the most time on, because it is where a cost dashboard turns into something dishonest.

It measures nine costs a business buys in, plus inflation rates. **That is not the cost of doing business.** There is no rent, no wages, no land, no taxes, no levies and no stock in it.

It does **not** predict whether a company will make or lose money, and it does **not** identify the best Nigerian state for business. A cheaper jurisdiction is not a better one. Whether a business does well somewhere also depends on customer demand, purchasing power, market size, competition, rent, wages, supplier access and infrastructure. None of that is in this data.

Those limits are not a footnote. They are stored as data in both tools, shown on two pages of each, and the build fails if a page ever claims otherwise. A language check walks every string in the report and refuses words like "best state" or "cheaper place to do business" before anything can ship.

## How I know it is right

**110 of 110 analysis checks pass**, across three separate passes. The database has its own independent audit at **245 of 245**, which re-parses all 17 source CSV files from scratch rather than trusting what was loaded.

Each dashboard is validated against the file itself, not against the build log:

- **The Excel workbook: 105 structural and reconciliation checks, plus 25 Microsoft Excel compatibility checks.** Every headline figure is recalculated and compared against the committed evidence file behind it. The workbook's hash is taken before and after validation and must be identical, which is the proof the validator is only reading.
- **The Power BI report: 89 checks.** The validator opens the project in Power BI Desktop, deletes the data cache so the refresh is genuine, and then interrogates the live engine directly.

That paranoia was earned. An early version of the workbook shipped 20 invalid numeric values buried in its XML, and every build log had called it a success. A build log saying "written" is not evidence.

Nothing is quietly patched. Eleven government releases that were due and never published stay missing and are named on the page. Where a year-on-year figure has no comparison month, the line breaks rather than bridging the gap.

## Built with

Python and pandas for the cleaning pipelines. PostgreSQL for the model and the analytical mart. SQL for the analysis. Power BI, as a text-based project rather than a binary file. Excel, generated and validated by script. Git throughout, with every stage committed as it was proved.

The repository holds the pipelines, the schema, the analysis scripts, the validation suites and both dashboards: [github.com/Qui-vira/nigeria-business-cost-intelligence](https://github.com/Qui-vira/nigeria-business-cost-intelligence).

## Why this matters to you

Most dashboards are built to look certain. This one is built to be checkable, and it says out loud where it stops.

If you need data work where the numbers survive someone actually testing them, this is what that looks like.
